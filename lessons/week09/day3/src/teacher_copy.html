Essential questionHow does the contrast between Anne’s tone and the mood of the final scene develop the theme of the play?
Learning targetI can develop an arguable theme and explain how the contrast between Anne’s tone and the mood of the final scene supports that theme using specific evidence.
The distinction · post it, keep it up all periodTOPIC ≠ PLOT SUMMARY ≠ ADVICE ≠ THEME. A theme is an arguable sentence about life or people beyond this one play, developed by the text and proved with evidence. TONE + MOOD → CONTRAST → THEME. The sorting question all period: “Is that a topic, advice, or an arguable idea?”

Get them here
Packet pp. 1–2, Box 02. Doc cam on your packet. Slide 5.
Say while doing
(point to the chips above the ladder) “Three things that are not theme. A topic is one word. A plot summary tells me what happened. Advice tells me what to do. A theme is an arguable idea about life or people, beyond this one play, that the text develops and evidence can prove.”
Model · climb
“Step one, topic. One word: hope. That’s what the play is about, not what it says. Step two, what the play says: ‘The play is about holding onto hope.’ Closer, but it’s still only this play. Step three, I climb to an idea about people: ‘The playwrights argue that hope is both precious and unbearably fragile in the face of evil.’ PRATHER Somebody could disagree with that. That’s how I know it’s arguable.”
Theme vs. moral
“‘Never give up hope’ is a bumper sticker. PRATHER That sounds nice, but nice is not enough. Can the text actually prove it? A theme needs complexity and evidence. Listen to this one: ‘the playwrights suggest that hope survives even those it cannot save.’ PRATHER That’s a theme this text can actually evidence.”
Scholars do
Follow along in the model column. They don’t fill in My Ladder yet.
Contrast → theme
(Slide 6, point to the dark bar) “If Anne’s hopeful tone stood alone, the theme would be simple. If the devastating mood stood alone, the theme would be despair. The playwrights force the two together, and the theme lives in that collision.” PRATHER
Ask
“What idea about people survives the contrast?” PRATHER Wait. Take one or two. Don’t answer it yourself. “Hold that. Don’t write it yet.”
Check

1 · Get them here
Purple strip, p. 2. Slide 7. “Here’s a candidate somebody might write: ‘Never give up hope.’ Read it with me.”
2 · Silent think
“Don’t write. Don’t talk. Is it a topic, advice, or an arguable idea? How would you turn it into a real theme? Thumb on your chest when you’ve got your ‘First I…’” Wait the full 30–45 seconds. No cold call yet.
3 · Scholar thinks aloud
“[Name], talk us through how you would turn that into a real theme statement. Start with ‘First I…’”
Listen for
“First I checked whether it was a topic, advice, or an idea, and it’s advice because it tells you what to do. Then I noticed Anne never gave up, but it didn’t save her. So I decided to make it about what hope does, not what you should do, because she says ‘in spite of everything’ and we know what happened.”
Press, don’t answer
“Is that a topic, advice, or an arguable idea?” · “Does it apply beyond Anne?” · “Could a reasonable person disagree?” · “Can Tuesday’s tone/mood evidence actually support it?” If they stall: “What did you notice first?” → “What part are you sure about?” Stay with the same scholar. Don’t rescue.
4 · Second scholar
“Who can build on, revise, or challenge that theme statement?” Stem: “I would revise it to ___ because ___.”
Listen for
A revision that adds complexity or ties it to evidence: “I’d revise it to ‘hope can survive even when it can’t save someone,’ because Anne still believes in people and we just heard she died in Belsen.” → “That’s arguable, and Tuesday’s map can prove it.”
5 · Release
“Good. That’s the process I want running in your head: name what it is, push it past this one play, make sure somebody could disagree, then check it against your evidence. Your ladder. Go.” Release immediately.
